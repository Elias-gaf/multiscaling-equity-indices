0 — Overview

What this project is, and why it exists

The project asks one question: do emerging stock markets behave differently from developed ones at a structural level — not just "more volatile", but differently shaped across time scales — and if so, is that difference real, or a statistical illusion produced by a few extreme days?

Concretely: it estimates how the statistics of price moves change depending on how hard you weight large moves versus small ones, for 22 countries' stock indices over nearly three decades. It follows a method published by Prof. Tiziana Di Matteo — who teaches on the KCL programme this project was built for — and then does something most replications don't: it tries hard to break its own headline result, and reports honestly where it breaks.

Purpose

This document exists to make every link in that chain legible, in order, with nothing assumed from a previous section left uncashed. Each section below opens by naming the open question left by the one before it, so the reasoning reads as one continuous argument rather than a list of techniques.

Why it's useful

  • It demonstrates exactly the gap the project was built to close. A weak undergraduate pure-mathematics transcript doesn't disprove mathematical maturity — a project that validates its own estimator, questions its own null hypothesis, and reports a negative result honestly, does that work instead.
  • It's a worked example of a general skill: not trusting your own measurement. See §13 for how this same instinct shows up in three earlier, unrelated projects.
  • The specific finding is genuinely interesting — and, as you'll see by §9, more fragile than it first looks.
1 — Foundations

The raw material: prices, logs, and increments

Everything starts from a daily closing price for each index, back to 1998 where available (~7,000 trading days). The project never works with the raw price P(t) directly — it works with the log price, X(t) = ln P(t).

Why logs, not prices — and why it isn't just convention

A move from 100 to 110 and a move from 1000 to 1100 are "the same" 10% change, but as raw numbers they're 10 and 100 — not comparable, and everything below depends on being able to compare moves across markets and decades. Logs turn multiplicative change (percent moves compound) into additive change: X(t+τ) − X(t) ≈ the return over the gap τ. That's not a technicality — without it, a crash in a cheap, low-priced index and a crash in an expensive one would look like different-sized numbers purely from the price level, for reasons that have nothing to do with risk.

The quantity the whole project is built on is the increment over a gap of τ days:

The one object everything else is a function ofΔXτ(t)  =  X(t + τ) − X(t)

τ (tau) is a time scale — one day, five days, nineteen days. The entire question "is this market multiscaling?" reduces to: does the statistical spread of ΔXτ grow with τ in the same way, regardless of how you measure "spread"? That's the next section — and "how you measure spread" is doing more work in that sentence than it looks like.

2 — The engine

The structure function, and what "q" actually does

The central object, for every market and every scale τ, is the q-th order structure function:

Structure functionSq(τ)  =  ⟨ |ΔXτ(t)|q ⟩t

In words: take every τ-day price move in the whole history, take its absolute size, raise it to the power q, and average over all of them (the ⟨ ⟩t notation just means "average over every starting day t"). q is an exponent — and the entire reason it's in this formula at all is that changing it changes whose behaviour the average is reporting on, without ever removing any day from the calculation.

What raising to the power q is actually doing — and what it isn't

The gap worth closing before anything else

It's tempting to read "q picks out extreme days" as meaning the method sorts days into two bins — "ordinary" and "extreme" — and looks at one bin or the other. It never does this. Every single day is in the average at every value of q. What changes is each day's weight in that average, continuously, as q moves. A day twice as large as another isn't "promoted to extreme" at some threshold — it simply counts for more, by an amount that grows smoothly as q increases. There is no categorisation step anywhere in this method; there is only reweighting. Holding onto that is what makes the next six sections cohere, rather than reading as "first sort days into buckets, then do statistics on the buckets" — which is a different (and wrong) method.

Here's the reweighting made concrete, on seven toy days — six ordinary moves and one large one:

Show it with real arithmetic on five numbers

Take five toy moves: 0.3, −0.5, 0.2, −4.0, 0.4 (the fourth is a crash day). Structure function at q=1 is the mean absolute value:

S₁ = (0.3+0.5+0.2+4.0+0.4)/5 = 5.4/5 = 1.08

The crash day's own contribution is 4.0/5.4 = 74% of that total. Now at q=4, each value is raised to the 4th power first:

0.3⁴=0.0081    0.5⁴=0.0625    0.2⁴=0.0016    4.0⁴=256    0.4⁴=0.0256
sum = 256.098   →   crash day's share = 256/256.098 = 99.96%

The other four numbers together now contribute a twentieth of a percent. Nothing was removed from the sum — their contribution simply became negligible. That's the entire mechanism, run by hand.

This is why the project scans q from 0.5 up to 4 in steps of 0.25 (fifteen values) rather than picking one: a single q only tells you about one slice of "how much weight you've decided to give extreme moves." Scanning q is how you find out whether the whole distribution of move sizes reweights the same way as q grows, or not — which is exactly what the next section turns that scan into a number for.

3 — The scaling law

The Hurst exponent, and the generalised H(q)

§2 fixed one scale, τ, and scanned q. This section does the opposite: fix q, and ask how S_q(τ) changes as τ itself grows — because the actual claim being tested is about time, not just about which moments are reliable.

The assumption is that S_q(τ) scales as a power law in τ:

Scaling assumptionSq(τ)  ~  τ q·H(q)

which, taking logs of both sides, becomes a straight line — and this is the step that turns an abstract "scales as a power law" into something you can actually fit with a ruler:

Linearised — this is literally what the code fitslog Sq(τ)  =  const  +  q·H(q) · log τ

So H(q) is nothing more exotic than the slope of a straight line on a log–log plot, divided by q to undo the exponent from the structure function. Two numbers you already have an intuition for sit at the edges:

  • H = 0.5 — a plain random walkeach day's move is independent of every other day's — the textbook "nothing interesting is happening" case. Moves at different scales are statistically independent.
  • H > 0.5 — persistentan up-move tends to be followed by more up-move: trending behaviour. H < 0.5 — anti-persistentmoves tend to reverse: mean-reverting behaviour.

Monofractal vs. multifractal — the shape that matters

Compute H(q) across the whole q-range from §2 and you get a curve, not a number. Its shape is the entire point:

Flat H(q) means one number describes price moves at every scale of "how extreme" — monofractal. A falling H(q) means ordinary days and extreme days are governed by different exponents — multiscaling (the project uses "multiscaling" and "multifractal" interchangeably, following Di Matteo's own paper). This is the point you've already got. The open question §4 answers: a whole curve is hard to rank 22 countries by — how do you turn "the curve falls" into one comparable number per market?

4 — One summary number

Multiscaling strength, B

Take the H(q) curve from §3 and fit another straight line through it — the same kind of least-squares fit as before, just one level up (first we fit a line to get each H(q) point; now we fit a line through those points):

The summary statisticH(q)  =  A  −  B·q
Why fit a straight line through H(q) rather than, say, just comparing H(1) to H(4)?

You could — and the project also computes that simpler version, D = H(1) − H(4), as a cross-check (next box). But a single pair of points throws away the other thirteen q-values computed in §2–3, and is more sensitive to noise in exactly those two points. Fitting a line uses all fifteen points at once, the same logic as using more data to estimate anything: the standard errorroughly, how much the estimate would wobble if you reran the measurement on a fresh sample — smaller is more trustworthy on a line fit through fifteen points is smaller than on a difference between two of them.

B is the sign-flipped slope of that fit. B = 0 is a flat curve — monofractal. Larger B means a more steeply falling H(q) — stronger multiscaling. This single number is what gets ranked, bootstrapped, and compared between developed and emerging markets in every chart from §8 onward. Nothing about computing B tells you whether it's trustworthy, though — a slope fit through fifteen noisy points can produce a confident-looking number even when the underlying curve isn't really falling at all. That's the open question the next two sections exist to answer, from two different directions: §5 asks "does this method even work, on cases where we already know the answer?", and §6 asks "did the specific choices made while fitting (how many τ, how many q) bias the number?"

A second, simpler proxy — used as a cross-check, not the headline

The code also computes D = H(1) − H(2) (confusingly named h1_minus_h4 in the source, from an earlier version that used q=4 — it still returns H(1) minus whatever the second argument is), following Di Matteo's own paper directly. B and D agree on every market in this dataset, which is itself a small piece of evidence that the finding isn't an artefact of one particular way of summarising the curve.

5 — Trust, then verify

Why the estimator is validated before it's trusted on real data

§4 left an open question: a confident-looking B could come from a method that's simply wrong. Before H(q) is estimated on a single real index, it's run on three synthetic processes whose true H(q) is known exactly — written down by a mathematician, not estimated — because an estimator that returns a plausible number is not the same thing as one that returns the right number, and at n≈7,000 daily observations, a biased estimator can still look entirely believable.

Two things this buys, stated plainly:

  • A null band. On a genuine random walk at this sample size, B exceeds 0.0046 only 5% of the time by chance alone. Any market's B has to clear that bar before "multiscaling" means anything more than noise — this is the dashed line you'll see on the ranking chart in §8.
  • A known, constant bias. When multiscaling is really there, the estimator recovers only about 66% of the true strength — at both tested levels, which is what makes it a calibration factor rather than an unpredictable error. Every B reported on real markets later is a lower bound, not a point estimate.
Where does the 66% actually come from?

The multifractal random walk is built from a log-correlated Gaussian volatility field, generated by an FFT trick called circulant embeddinga standard way to generate correlated random noise fast, by using the Fourier transform — a computational trick, not a modelling choice. That trick requires clipping a handful of mathematically-required-to-be-impossible negative eigenvalueshere: numbers that come out of the Fourier-transform step and should always be positive for the maths to be consistent — a few come out tiny and negative purely from floating-point rounding that appear only because of floating-point rounding — standard practice, but it very slightly throttles the simulated volatility correlations. That's the direct, traceable cause of the third of the signal the estimator doesn't recover.

6 — A choice with consequences

The fitting range: why τmax = 19 and q up to 4, not further

§3 fit a straight line through log S_q(τ) vs log τ — but a straight line needs a window of τ-values to be fit over, and nobody has said yet how that window gets chosen. This section is that choice, made explicit, because both ends of it trade bias against variance.

τ (the time-scale window, 1 to 19 trading days)

Too narrow a window (small τmax) and the slope is noisy — too few points to pin it down reliably, so the fitted line swings a lot from one resample to the next. Too wide a window and you risk fitting straight through a point where the power law has already broken down (price behaviour over one day and over three months needn't share the same exponent). Try it below on a toy example built the same shape as the real data, deliberately bending upward in its last few points to simulate exactly that kind of regime change:

Drag below 6 points: the line swings wildly between drags of similar data — high variance. Drag past 12: the fitted line starts tilting upward to chase the last few points, which were deliberately built to bend away from the true trend — that's bias from over-extending the window. The real project's choice of 19 is the same trade-off, made on real data instead of this toy one.

q (the moment range, 0.5 to 4)

This range has a sharper reason behind it, and it's the single most important methodological fact in the whole project — important enough that the whole of §7 is built around unpacking it: sample momentsa "moment" here just means an average of values raised to some power — the mean is the 1st moment, variance comes from the 2nd, kurtosis from the 4th of a heavy-tailed distribution become statistically unreliable as q grows, because a moment of order q is dominated by the single largest observation once q exceeds roughly the number of finite moments the underlying distribution actually has. With fat-tailed daily returns, that ceiling arrives well before q=4. The project doesn't just pick q≤4 and hope — it separately re-estimates everything restricted to q ≤ 2, where this problem is far smaller, as an explicit check rather than a buried assumption.

7 — The real question

Kurtosis, and "temporal structure" — dynamics or just a fat distribution?

Here's the open thread from §2 and §6 coming together: §2 showed that high q lets one extreme day dominate the average; §6 showed that this is a real statistical problem, not a minor one. This section asks the question that follows directly: if one extreme day can dominate the structure function on its own, does a "falling H(q) curve" actually require any real time-dependent behaviour at all — or can a single bad day fake the whole signature?

What kurtosis measures

Kurtosisa number describing how much of a distribution's variance comes from rare extreme values versus everyday ones; 0 for a Normal distribution, much higher for "fat-tailed" data is the standardised fourth moment of a distribution — it measures how much of the variance comes from rare, extreme values rather than everyday ones:

Excess kurtosis (Normal distribution = 0, by convention)Kurt[X]  =  E[(X − μ)4] / σ4  −  3
Why the fourth power, and why subtract 3?

The fourth power is what makes kurtosis sensitive specifically to the tails rather than the middle of a distribution: a value twice as far from the mean contributes sixteen times as much to this average (2⁴=16) as a value at average distance, whereas in an ordinary variance (second power) it only contributes four times as much. That's a much sharper spotlight on rare, extreme values than variance gives you. The −3 is just a convention that makes a Normal (bell-curve) distribution read as exactly 0, so positive numbers always mean "fatter-tailed than Normal" and you don't have to remember that a Normal distribution's raw kurtosis is 3.

Daily equity returns almost never have kurtosis near 0 — the 22 markets here range from 4.3 (Taiwan — only mildly fat-tailed) to 85.9 (Malaysia — a single historical event, the 1998 Asian-crisis capital controls, dominates the entire 28-year sample).

That's the entire worry in one widget: the two quantities are mechanically linked, which is exactly why a high B needs to be checked against kurtosis before it's believed (§10), not just celebrated.

Why this threatens the entire headline finding

An iidindependent and identically distributed — each value is drawn separately, with no memory of any other value, from the same distribution every time sequence of returns — meaning each day's return is drawn independently, with zero memory of any other day — can manufacture a falling H(q) curve purely from the shape of its one-day distribution, with no real dynamics whatsoever.

The control that actually settles it

The project builds a random walk from an iid Student-t distribution (a textbook fat-tailed distribution, with a degrees of freedoma parameter of the Student-t distribution controlling how fat its tails are — lower means fatter tails and more extreme outliers parameter controlling exactly how fat the tails are) with zero temporal structure by construction. At df=5 it produces B ≈ 0.026; at df=3, B ≈ 0.069 — squarely inside the range measured on the real markets. A process with no time-dependent structure at all reproduces the headline effect just from having a fat-tailed distribution. So B alone cannot answer "dynamics or tails?" — which is exactly why §8–10 exist as separate, independent checks rather than one number being asked to do all the work.

This is also the exact instinct that recurs across the author's other work: in actuarial pension modelling, what matters is the propagated distribution's tail, not its centre; in football tracking data, the recurring question is whether an observed pattern is real structure or a measurement artefact. Same question, different field — see §13.

8 — Two different resampling tricks

Block bootstrap vs. shuffle surrogates — two tools for two different jobs

§7 leaves two separate things still needed: some way to say how uncertain a given B estimate is (so "B=0.078 for Malaysia" can come with an honest range, not a bare number), and some way to build the null band promised back in §5 directly from each market's own data, not just from an abstract random walk. Both bootstrap and shuffle resample the same return series — but for opposite purposes, and conflating them is the easiest way to misread this kind of analysis.

Block bootstrap
→ confidence interval

Cuts the return series into contiguous blocks (length ≈ n1/3 ≈ 19 days here) and resamples whole blocks, not individual days, then rebuilds a price path and re-estimates B — 500 times. Resampling blocks rather than single days is deliberate: it preserves short-range volatility clustering within a block while still injecting resampling randomness — which is what makes the resulting spread an honest confidence interval, rather than one that pretends consecutive days are independent when they demonstrably aren't.

Shuffle surrogates
→ null distribution

Randomly permutes individual days, completely destroying their original order. This keeps the marginal distributionthe distribution of individual values on their own, ignoring what order they came in — e.g. the exact same set of returns, just rearranged of returns exactly — same numbers, same kurtosis — while eliminating anything that depended on order. Whatever multiscaling survives this is definitionally not about dynamics.

Try both on the same 24-day toy series (shock fixed at 6)

Click Shuffle it a few times: the kurtosis number barely moves — same numbers, just reordered, which is exactly why the shuffle keeps the marginal distribution "exactly" as claimed above. Click Block-resample it a few times: the kurtosis number jitters more, because some days (including, sometimes, the shock day) now appear twice or not at all.

The twist that makes this project more than a replication

The standard sanity check in this literature is: shuffle the data, and if multiscaling survives, it must be a tail artefact rather than real dynamics. Here, every one of the 22 markets comes back with more multiscaling after shuffling than before. That looks damning — until the same test is run on the multifractal random walk from §5, whose multiscaling is multifractal by mathematical construction, genuinely real. Shuffling that process also increases its measured B (from 0.0119 to 0.0366 at λ²=0.04). Destroying volatility clustering isolates the large moves from their neighbours while leaving the heavy marginal distribution completely intact — and isolated large moves inflate this particular statistic regardless of whether the original structure was real. The plain shuffle is a marginal-matched benchmark, not a one-sided null test — which is exactly why the project reports the excess over the shuffled value (dB = B − B_shuffled) as a separate, more honest statistic, rather than treating "beats the shuffle" as the bar to clear.

9 — Comparing two groups

The Mann–Whitney U test, and what p = 0.0013 actually says

§8 produced, for every market, a B with a confidence interval and a shuffle-based null comparison. The remaining open question is the one the project was actually asked at the very top: are emerging markets as a group different from developed markets as a group? With 13 developed markets and 9 emerging ones, that's a two-group comparison — and the project deliberately doesn't reach for the most familiar tool (a t-test comparing two means).

Why not just compare the two group means with a t-test?

A t-test's p-value is only trustworthy if the quantity being compared is roughly Normally distributed within each group — and B has one extreme outlier (Malaysia) that distorts a mean-and-variance-based test badly. A tiny worked example makes this concrete: suppose "emerging" B values were 0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.90 (one wild outlier) and "developed" were 0.01, 0.01, 0.01, 0.01, 0.01. The emerging mean is dragged up to 0.19 by that single number, making the group look dramatically different by a t-test — but four of the five emerging values are barely different from developed at all. A rank-baseda test that only uses the order (1st smallest, 2nd smallest, …) of the combined values, not their actual sizes — so one wildly large number can't dominate the result the way it can dominate a mean test instead asks how often, across all pairs, the emerging value beats the developed one — and that 0.90 only ever "wins" the same number of pairwise comparisons as a merely-large number would, however enormous it actually is. Mann–Whitney is specifically built to not be distorted this way.

Mann–Whitney asks a purely rank-based question:

What the test actually computesIf you pick one emerging-market B and one developed-market B at random,
how often is the emerging one larger?

Under the null hypothesisthe deliberately boring assumption being tested against — here, "the two groups are really drawn from the same distribution, with no true difference" (no real difference between the groups), that probability should be 50%. The test's p-valuethe probability of seeing a result this extreme (or more extreme) if the null hypothesis were actually true — NOT the probability that the null hypothesis itself is true is the probability of seeing a split this lopsided, or more lopsided, if the null hypothesis were true.

Reading p = 0.0013 correctly

It means: if emerging and developed markets really had identically distributed multiscaling, you'd see a split this extreme (9 emerging markets ranking this far above 13 developed ones) only about 13 times in 10,000 random re-splits. It does not mean "there's a 0.13% chance the null is true" (a common and wrong rephrasing), and it says nothing at all about why the groups differ — which is precisely the question §7–8 exist to chase down separately, and §10 checks one specific candidate answer for.

The same test, run five different ways, is how "does the gap survive?" gets answered without relying on a single number:

That pattern across five deliberately adversarial versions of the same test — not any single p-value — is the actual finding.

10 — Does kurtosis explain it away?

Controlling for kurtosis: a two-variable regression

§9's tests compare two groups but don't separate out why a market scores high — being emerging, or just having a fat-tailed return distribution, which §7 showed can inflate B on its own with zero dynamics. A regressiona way of asking how much each of several possible explanations individually contributes to an outcome, holding the others fixed can hold one of those explanations fixed while asking about the other:

The regression actually runB  =  β0  +  β1·(excess kurtosis)  +  β2·(1 if emerging, else 0)  +  error
What a coefficient, a t-statistic and R² each actually mean here

β₁ and β₂ are the model's best estimate of how much B moves per unit change in kurtosis, and how much B shifts for an emerging market versus a developed one of the same kurtosis, respectively. A t-statistica coefficient divided by its own uncertainty (standard error) — a way of asking "is this effect big relative to how unsure we are about it?" is simply that coefficient divided by its own standard error; as a rough rule of thumb, |t| above about 2 is the conventional bar for "probably not noise" at this sample size. R²the fraction of the variation in the outcome (here, B across the 22 markets) that the model's predictors jointly explain — 0 means no explanatory power, 1 means perfect = 0.80 means these two predictors together explain 80% of the variation in B across the 22 markets.

If β₂ (the emerging-market dummy) is still clearly non-zero after kurtosis is accounted for, that's evidence the developed/emerging split carries information kurtosis alone doesn't. It survives at t = 3.6, p = 0.002, R² = 0.80 — kurtosis is doing real work (it's correlated with B, as §7 would predict, and the §8 scatter below shows it directly), but the emerging/developed distinction adds something on top of it rather than being swallowed by it. This is the closest thing in the whole project to a direct answer to "is it just the fat tails?" — and the answer is mostly, but not entirely.

12 — Code, mapped to concept

Every function, and the idea it implements

The estimator lives in src/ghe.py (≈150 lines) and the validation processes in src/synthetic.py. Every function that does conceptual work, mapped to the section above that motivates it.

structure_function(X, q, taus)  /  structure_functions(X, qs, taus)

Computes S_q(τ) = mean(|X[t+τ] − X[t]|^q) for one q or every q in qs at once. This is §2 turned into array code.

Why it matters: everything downstream — H(q), B, the bootstrap, the shuffle test — is just this function called repeatedly on resampled or permuted versions of the same series.

_ols(x, y)  /  _ols_many(x, Y)

Ordinary least squares — the plain straight-line fit, by closed-form formula rather than a library call, because the slope is wanted for many rows at once.

Why it matters: the literal mechanism behind both nested fits in §3 and §4 — the whole method is two straight-line fits, one inside the other.

generalised_hurst(X, qs, tau_max, tau_min)

Runs structure_functions, takes logs, calls _ols_many, divides by q to recover H(q) (§3).

Why it matters: this is the estimator itself — the one validated against known processes in §5 before being trusted on real markets.

multiscaling_strength(qs, H)  /  h1_minus_h4(qs, H)

Fits H(q)=A−Bq (§4) and returns B; the second reads off H(1)−H(2) by interpolation.

Why it matters: turns a 15-point curve into the single ranking number used in every chart in §11.

circular_block_bootstrap(increments, block, rng)  /  block_bootstrap(...)

Resamples whole blocks of daily increments with replacement, rebuilds a price path, re-estimates B — 500 times (§8).

Why it matters: the confidence whiskers on every bar in §11.1 come from here.

shuffle_surrogates(X, qs, tau_max, n_surr, seed)

Randomly permutes individual daily increments, rebuilds a price path, re-estimates B — 500 times — the null behind §7–9.

Why it matters: the function behind the single most important finding in the project — that shuffled real markets and a shuffled MRW both show more multiscaling than the unshuffled version (§8's twist).

brownian_motion, fractional_bm, mrw  — synthetic.py

Three generators of fake price series with mathematically known H(q), of increasing sophistication: plain cumulative Gaussian noise; FFT-correlated Gaussian noise for long-range dependence; Gaussian noise multiplied by a log-correlated random volatility field for genuine multifractality.

Why it matters: the known-answer test cases in §5 — without them, there's no way to know generalised_hurst is trustworthy before pointing it at real markets.

13 — Not a one-off

Where this builds on earlier work

The method is new; the moves this project makes are the same ones that show up across three earlier, unrelated-looking projects — the real argument for why this is evidence of transferable skill rather than a one-off replication.

Exeter group project
CMIP6 sea-ice modelling

Thirty-five climate models filtered to twenty by a confidence-interval test, combined by model selection and an emergent-constraint regression, rather than trusting any single model's projection. Same move as here: don't trust one estimate — build a band, filter by a statistical criterion, check the answer survives across choices (§6's τmax grid is the direct descendant of that instinct).

Actuarial work
stochastic pension modelling

The object of interest in multi-decade pension liability projection is the tail of the propagated distribution, not the central forecast. Same move as here: §7's whole argument is that the headline result lives in the tail moments (q>2), which is precisely why it needs the same suspicion an actuary gives a tail-driven number.

Lincoln City FC
tracking & recruitment data

With 76% of opposition ratings missing, the recurring problem was distinguishing real structure in high-frequency, many-agent data from measurement artefact. Same move as here: §7–8's apparatus — the iid Student-t control, the shuffle surrogate — answers exactly that question for markets instead of match data.

Three different domains, one discipline: before reporting a pattern, build something with a known answer, check the method recovers it, then ask what would make the pattern fake before asking what it means if it's real.
